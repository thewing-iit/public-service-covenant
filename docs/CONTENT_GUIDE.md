# Content Guide

## Editorial Mission

Public Service Covenant content should make civic commitments understandable, traceable, and fair. The product is not trying to entertain, persuade, or inflame. It is trying to help people inspect public promises with enough context to form responsible judgments.

Every page should answer the reader's quiet question: "How do we know?"

## Core Editorial Standards

Be clear:

- Use plain language
- Define unfamiliar terms
- Prefer concrete dates, names, and actions
- Summarize before adding detail

Be fair:

- Describe commitments in context
- Avoid loaded phrasing
- Distinguish delay from refusal
- Distinguish partial progress from completion

Be sourced:

- Attach sources to important claims
- Identify where a commitment was made
- Note when evidence is incomplete
- Keep source metadata visible

Be durable:

- Write for future readers
- Avoid references that depend on today's news cycle
- Use absolute dates instead of "recently" or "last week"
- Preserve change history when status shifts

## Voice and Tone

Use a calm civic voice. The tone should be direct, restrained, and helpful.

Prefer:

- "The city published a draft implementation plan on June 4, 2026."
- "The commitment remains in progress because funding has been approved, but construction has not started."
- "Available records do not show whether the office has selected a vendor."

Avoid:

- "The city finally admitted it had a plan."
- "This promise is clearly fake."
- "The implementation paradigm remains contingent on interagency alignment."

## Reading Level

Aim for clear public language. Most summaries should be understandable to a reader without policy expertise.

Guidelines:

- Use shorter sentences when explaining process
- Break complex sequences into steps
- Avoid unexplained acronyms
- Replace institutional jargon with everyday terms where accurate
- Keep technical or legal language only when precision requires it

## Commitment Titles

Commitment titles should be specific, neutral, and scannable.

Good:

- "Publish quarterly police misconduct data"
- "Create a public dashboard for street repair requests"
- "Fund 500 new supportive housing units"

Avoid:

- "Transparency"
- "Housing promise"
- "Mayor's bold safety pledge"

Title pattern:

Verb + object + scope when useful

Examples:

- "Expand evening library hours at five branches"
- "Release annual climate resilience spending report"
- "Establish independent review of procurement complaints"

## Summaries

Commitment summaries should explain what was promised, who made the promise, and why it matters.

Recommended structure:

1. Name the commitment.
2. Identify the responsible person or institution.
3. Mention the source and date.
4. Explain the practical public impact.

Example:

"During a May 14, 2026 council meeting, the mayor committed to publishing quarterly data on police misconduct complaints. The record would give residents a recurring view of complaint volume, investigation status, and outcomes."

## Status Explanations

Every status should include a short explanation. The explanation should name the evidence that supports the label.

Good:

"Status is in progress. The council approved funding on July 2, 2026, and the public works department published a procurement notice on July 18, 2026. No vendor award has been announced."

Avoid:

"Status is in progress because things are moving."

## Source Standards

Preferred sources:

- Official meeting minutes
- Public transcripts
- Government reports
- Budget documents
- Legislative records
- Candidate policy pages archived with date
- Public speeches or interviews with reliable recordings
- Public agency dashboards
- Court or administrative filings
- Credible journalistic reporting when primary records are unavailable

Use caution with:

- Social media posts without archival capture
- Campaign summaries without exact wording
- Unsourced secondary claims
- Advocacy materials that paraphrase commitments
- Anonymous submissions

Do not use as sole evidence:

- Rumor
- Private messages without consent and verification
- Edited clips without context
- Claims that cannot be traced to a public record

## Citation Metadata

Capture as much as possible:

- Source title
- Publisher or institution
- Author or speaker, if known
- Publication or event date
- Access date
- URL
- Archive URL, if available
- Page, timestamp, section, or agenda item
- Notes about transcript quality or excerpt boundaries

## Quoting and Paraphrasing

Use short quotes only when exact wording matters. Prefer paraphrase for readability, but keep a link to the source.

When quoting:

- Preserve meaning
- Avoid cherry-picking
- Include enough context to prevent distortion
- Use quotation marks only for exact language

When paraphrasing:

- Do not intensify or soften the original claim
- Avoid adding motives
- Note ambiguity when the source is vague

## Status Language

Use status labels consistently:

- Proposed: no clear implementation action yet
- In progress: action has begun
- Completed: evidence supports fulfillment
- Delayed: expected timeline has slipped
- Blocked: outside dependency is preventing progress
- Changed: scope or terms have materially shifted
- Withdrawn: commitment is no longer being pursued
- Unknown: evidence is insufficient

Do not invent new labels casually. If a label does not fit, document the edge case and update the design/specification intentionally.

## Dates

Use absolute dates:

- "August 3, 2026"
- "Q3 2026" when only quarter is known
- "2026" when only year is known

Avoid relative dates:

- "today"
- "recently"
- "last month"
- "soon"

If the source gives a relative date, translate it where possible and note uncertainty.

## Corrections

Corrections should be visible, dated, and plain.

Correction note should include:

- What was wrong
- What changed
- Date corrected
- Source or reason for correction

Do not silently overwrite meaningful errors.

## Sensitive Content

Civic accountability can involve allegations, misconduct, personal hardship, and conflict. Handle sensitive material with care.

Guidelines:

- Avoid unnecessary personal details
- Do not publish private contact information
- Attribute allegations clearly
- Distinguish allegations from findings
- Avoid sensational framing
- Consider whether a detail is necessary for public understanding

## SEO and Metadata

Page titles should be descriptive:

- "Publish quarterly police misconduct data | Public Service Covenant"

Descriptions should summarize the record:

- "Track the public commitment to publish quarterly police misconduct data, including source, status, evidence, and review history."

Avoid clickbait, partisan keywords, or inflated claims.

## Review Checklist

Before publishing a commitment, confirm:

- Title is specific and neutral
- Summary is understandable
- Source metadata is complete enough
- Status is supported by evidence
- Status explanation is dated
- Ambiguity is named
- No unsupported motive claims are included
- Sensitive details are necessary and handled carefully
- Internal notes are not exposed publicly

## Content Governance

At minimum, each published record should have:

- Author or maintainer
- Last reviewed date
- Source trail
- Correction path

Future editorial workflow should include review before publication for real-world political claims.
