# Branding

## Brand Essence

Public Service Covenant is a calm civic accountability brand. It should feel serious, public-minded, readable, and useful. The brand should never feel like a campaign, a watchdog stunt, a government seal, or a social media outrage product.

Core brand idea:

> Public promises deserve a public record.

## Name

Primary name: Public Service Covenant

Short forms:

- The Covenant
- PSC

Use the full name in first references, formal contexts, page titles, and external descriptions. Use short forms only after the full name has been introduced.

## Positioning

Public Service Covenant helps communities track public commitments with source-backed records, plain language summaries, and transparent status updates.

The project sits between civic education, public records, journalism support, and accountability tooling. It should be useful to people who want evidence and context, not just a quick emotional verdict.

## Tagline Options

Primary:

- Public promises. Public record.

Alternates:

- Track commitments. Preserve trust.
- A clearer record of public service.
- Civic promises, made inspectable.
- Accountability without the noise.

Use the primary tagline unless a specific page needs a softer or more descriptive line.

## Audience Promise

For residents:

You can see what was promised, where it came from, and what has happened since.

For journalists and researchers:

You can inspect sources, dates, evidence, and status changes without rebuilding the record from scratch.

For public servants:

You can make commitments in a format that respects complexity and makes progress legible.

For civic organizations:

You can organize around specific public commitments with a shared reference point.

## Personality

The brand should be:

- Clear
- Fair
- Civic
- Patient
- Evidence-minded
- Direct
- Accessible
- Durable

The brand should not be:

- Partisan
- Snarky
- Ceremonial
- Bureaucratic
- Alarmist
- Self-important
- Overly academic
- Decorative for its own sake

## Voice

Write like a careful public editor. Prefer plain words, active sentences, and specific nouns.

Good:

- "The commitment was made during a public council meeting on May 14, 2026."
- "No implementation plan has been published yet."
- "The status is listed as in progress because funding has been approved, but work has not started."

Avoid:

- "A bold new era of accountable democratic renewal begins here."
- "Officials failed the people again."
- "This initiative operationalizes participatory governance outcomes."

## Visual Identity Direction

The visual identity should signal trust through layout and consistency rather than through patriotic decoration.

Recommended qualities:

- Strong typography
- Generous but efficient spacing
- High contrast text
- Muted civic colors with one or two purposeful accents
- Tables, timelines, filters, and source cards designed for scanning
- Icons used sparingly for status, source type, external links, and navigation

Avoid:

- Flags, eagles, seals, gavels, marble textures, or generic government motifs
- Red-versus-blue partisan palettes
- Excessive gradients
- Decorative badges that imply authority the project does not have
- Stock photos of crowds or capitol buildings unless they are relevant and sourced

## Color Direction

The palette should feel public, grounded, and readable. Recommended foundation:

- Ink: near-black for primary text
- Paper: warm white or neutral white for main backgrounds
- Civic blue: restrained action and link color
- Moss or green: verified progress and positive completion
- Amber: pending, delayed, or needs review
- Red: errors or serious blockers only
- Slate: dividers, metadata, and secondary text

Do not let the interface become a single blue or slate theme. Accountability work benefits from restrained variety that helps users distinguish status, action, metadata, and evidence.

Recommended logo concepts:

- A simple wordmark
- A ledger or record mark
- A bracket, column, or line motif that suggests structured public memory
- A civic but non-governmental symbol

Avoid marks that imitate official seals or suggest endorsement by a public agency.

## Messaging Pillars

Public memory:

- Promises should not vanish after the speech, debate, or press release.

Plain language:

- Civic records should be understandable without insider knowledge.

Evidence:

- Claims should be linked to public sources.

Fairness:

- Status should reflect real progress, delay, uncertainty, and context.

Usefulness:

- The product should help people act, ask better questions, and follow public work over time.

## Sample Boilerplate

Short description:

Public Service Covenant is a civic accountability project that turns public service promises into source-backed records people can read, track, and revisit.

Medium description:

Public Service Covenant helps residents, journalists, civic groups, and public servants follow public commitments over time. Each record connects a promise to its source, status, evidence, and review history so accountability can be clear without becoming simplistic.

## Naming Conventions

Use "commitment" for a public promise that is specific enough to track.

Use "source" for the public record that proves the commitment was made or updated.

Use "evidence" for documents, actions, votes, budgets, plans, or public statements relevant to status.

Use "status" for the current editorial assessment of progress.

Use "review" for maintainer evaluation of evidence and status.
