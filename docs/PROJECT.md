# Public Service Covenant

## Project Overview

Public Service Covenant is a civic technology project for turning public service promises into clear, durable, and reviewable commitments. The project is intended to help candidates, officeholders, public servants, community groups, journalists, and residents understand what has been pledged, what evidence exists, and what progress has been made.

The product should not behave like a campaign site, a partisan scorecard, or a rumor feed. Its job is to create a trustworthy public record: plain language commitments, source-backed context, transparent status updates, and a consistent way to compare stated intentions with public action.

## Product Purpose

Public Service Covenant exists to make public service accountable without making accountability performative. It should help people answer practical civic questions:

- What exactly was promised?
- Who made the commitment?
- When and where was it made?
- What public evidence supports the commitment?
- Has progress been reported?
- What remains unresolved?
- How can residents follow the issue without becoming experts in bureaucracy?

The project should lower the cost of civic understanding. It should make complex commitments easier to inspect while avoiding the false simplicity of slogans, grades, or outrage-first commentary.

## Primary Audiences

Residents and voters need a calm, readable way to see what public servants promised and whether public action matches those promises.

Community organizations need structured records they can cite, share, and use when organizing around public priorities.

Journalists and researchers need traceable sources, dates, quotes, and status histories.

Candidates and public officials need a public format for making commitments that are specific enough to be evaluated.

Maintainers need a project structure that supports careful editorial review, defensible sourcing, and steady feature growth.

## Project Principles

Clarity over spectacle. The product should make commitments understandable before it makes them beautiful.

Evidence over assertion. Important claims should be grounded in cited public records, documents, statements, or verified updates.

Fairness over gotchas. The product should recognize nuance, changed conditions, partial progress, and honest constraints.

Public usefulness over insider fluency. Civic content should be readable by people who do not already know the names of agencies, procedures, or legislative mechanisms.

Durability over virality. The core record should remain useful months or years after the political moment has passed.

## Near-Term Scope

The next stage should replace the default starter page with a small but complete product slice. A strong first version would include:

- A home page that explains the covenant concept in plain language
- A browsable list of sample commitments
- A commitment detail page with status, source, date, and evidence sections
- A content model that can later move to a CMS or database
- A visual direction that feels civic, calm, accessible, and serious

The first implementation should favor a small number of excellent examples over a broad but shallow catalog.

## Out of Scope for the Foundation Stage

The foundation stage should not attempt to solve every public accountability problem. The following should wait until the project has a working core:

- Real-time political news aggregation
- Automated truth scoring
- User-generated public accusations
- Partisan rankings
- Donation, campaign, or endorsement workflows
- Social-media-style engagement features
- Complex admin permissions
- Large-scale data ingestion

## Success Criteria

The project is succeeding when a visitor can understand a public commitment without needing background knowledge, can inspect the sources behind it, and can tell what has changed over time.

The documentation is succeeding when contributors can make product, design, content, and engineering decisions without repeatedly reinventing the project's purpose.
