# Public Service Covenant Manifesto

## The Civic Promise

Public service is built on promises. Some promises are formal: laws, budgets, appointments, executive orders, policy platforms. Others are spoken in town halls, interviews, debates, community meetings, and public letters. All of them shape public trust.

Public Service Covenant begins from a simple belief: promises made in public should remain visible in public.

That does not mean every promise is easy to fulfill. Conditions change. Institutions are complicated. Good faith efforts can stall. New evidence can reshape what responsible action looks like. Accountability becomes useful only when it can hold these realities together: clarity, context, evidence, and memory.

## Why This Matters

Democratic life is weakened when public commitments disappear into fragments. A resident may remember a promise, but not the date. A journalist may know a source, but not the status. An organizer may track progress privately, but lack a durable public reference. An official may have taken meaningful steps, but those steps are hidden behind administrative language.

When the record is scattered, trust becomes a matter of mood. People rely on loyalty, cynicism, personality, or the loudest recent claim.

This project is an answer to that problem. It is a public memory system for civic commitments.

## What We Believe

We believe public commitments should be specific enough to inspect.

We believe residents deserve plain language explanations of decisions made in their name.

We believe public servants should be judged by evidence, not by selective clips or permanent suspicion.

We believe a promise is not fulfilled because someone says it is, and not broken simply because it is unfinished.

We believe context is not an excuse. It is part of the record.

We believe civic technology should make people more capable, not more dependent on expert intermediaries.

We believe the best public tools feel calm, careful, and trustworthy.

## The Covenant

A covenant is stronger than a slogan. It implies a relationship, an obligation, and a record that outlasts a news cycle.

In this project, a covenant means:

- Public commitments are written down.
- The source of each commitment is visible.
- The people affected by the commitment are considered.
- Progress is tracked over time.
- Status changes are explained.
- Evidence is preserved.
- Uncertainty is named instead of hidden.

The covenant is not only between public officials and residents. It also guides the maintainers of this project. We owe users restraint, accuracy, accessibility, and intellectual honesty.

## What This Project Is Not

This is not a campaign platform.

This is not a partisan enforcement machine.

This is not a replacement for journalism, organizing, public records work, or democratic deliberation.

This is not a feed designed to maximize outrage.

This is not a simplistic promise tracker that treats every delay as failure and every announcement as success.

This project should be firm without being cynical, clear without being reductive, and civic without being ceremonial.

## Editorial Commitments

Every important claim should be traceable.

Every status label should be explainable.

Every person or institution represented in the record should be treated with basic fairness.

Every user should be able to distinguish between verified facts, editorial summaries, pending questions, and interpretation.

When evidence is incomplete, the product should say so.

When a commitment changes, the product should preserve the history instead of silently replacing it.

When a mistake is found, the correction should be visible and dated.

## Design Commitments

The interface should reduce civic friction. It should help users compare, read, filter, cite, and revisit information without decorative overload.

Visual authority must come from structure, hierarchy, and restraint. It should not come from patriotic cliches, alarmist color, excessive badges, or faux official styling.

Accessibility is not a compliance afterthought. If public accountability is the purpose, the public must be able to use the tool.

## Technical Commitments

The system should be built in a way that supports public trust:

- Content should be reviewable.
- Data should have clear ownership and provenance.
- Status transitions should be auditable.
- Sources should remain attached to claims.
- Changes should be understandable to future maintainers.
- Privacy should be respected by default.

Technical convenience should not blur the line between sourced records and generated summaries.

## The Standard

Public Service Covenant should help people become more precise in their civic expectations. It should invite users away from vague disappointment and toward concrete questions:

- What was promised?
- What action followed?
- What evidence exists?
- What remains undone?
- Who is responsible for the next step?

A community that can ask those questions clearly is harder to mislead and easier to serve.
